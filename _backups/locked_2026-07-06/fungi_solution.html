An ordinary filter traps mold spores but doesn't kill them. They survive in the media, and the filter becomes a source. NanoFlashing carries a permanent positive polarity. It pulls the negatively charged spore onto the fiber and destroys the living cell on contact — by physical action, not chemistry. Almost none of what it catches survives to grow or shed back.

Researchers at Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School, in a study with funding from the U.S. National Institutes of Health, tested NanoFlashing the hard way. The standard mold test is gentle: a light dose of spores, laid on the material, left in still air, 28 days to see whether mold grows. This test challenged NanoFlashing with more than 100 million live, dry spores, driven in by fast-moving air in just 3 minutes — the way mold actually reaches a filter.
