# C-POLAR front page — provenance record

**Prepared for counsel. 10 August 2026, updated 11 August 2026.**

This is a factual record of where every part of the new front page came from, written by
the person who built it. **It is not a legal opinion and no lawyer has reviewed any of
it.** Its purpose is to put counsel in possession of the facts — including the two that
are least comfortable — before anything launches.

Four pages are discussed:

| | path | status |
|---|---|---|
| **v1** | `/cpolar/` | live now. Still loads the reference's compiled engine and about twenty of their asset files |
| **v2** | `/cpolar_v2/` | the hero on its own, built on our engine |
| **v4** | `/cpolar_v4/` | v1's page with only the hero replaced. Their 911 KB engine gone; their stylesheet and some of their JavaScript still present |
| **v5** | `/cpolar_v5/` | **v4 with everything of theirs removed.** The candidate to replace v1 |

---

## 1. The question that started this

A colleague looked at v1 and said it resembled a reference site closely enough to carry
legal risk if the page went public. That is the question this record serves.

## 3. The uncomfortable fact: I read their shader

I need to state this plainly rather than let it be discovered.

**What I did.** After several failed attempts to make the cloud look right, I opened the
reference's compiled JavaScript bundle and read the GLSL shader that draws their cloud,
and separately the one that shades their mountain. I did this to understand *how* the
effect is produced.

**What I learnt, and it was two things.** That a cloud there is drawn as a horizontal
edge whose height is displaced by scrolling noise, rather than as a pile of soft blobs;
and that the cloud material has depth testing disabled. Both are techniques.

**What I then did.** Wrote a new shader. My own hashed value noise, my own uniform
names, my own numbers, my own structure.

**The evidence.** Their cloud shader and mine were broken into statements, stripped of
comments and whitespace, and compared:

```
their cloud shader     35 statements
my cloud shader        37 statements
identical              1        —  "varying vec2 vUv"

their terrain shader vs my material code
identical              0
```

The single shared line is the most common declaration in GLSL; it appears in essentially
every three.js example and tutorial.

**What I believe, which is not a legal opinion.** Copyright protects expression rather
than method, so a technique understood and then independently written ought to be on the
right side of the line. I am aware that reading compiled code to learn from it is its
own question, that *Sega v. Accolade* addressed intermediate copying in the narrower
setting of interoperability, and that this was not interoperability. **Counsel should
form their own view. This is exactly the fact I did not want a lawyer to find out
later.**

## 4. The other uncomfortable fact: the pages still resemble each other

Both are pale and cool, both put a mountain on the right and the copy on the left, and
both move the camera as you scroll.

Measured — four frames from each, same browser, same size, same moments after load:

| | dark area | contrast | blueness | motion over 24s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| reference | 0.4% | 65 | 15.2 | 4.7 |
| v1 | 5.0% | 83 | 20.5 | 11.6 |
| v2 | ~10% | 81 | ~14 | ~2.5 |

Honestly stated: v2 was deliberately moved **toward** v1 during the build, at the
owner's direction, because v1 was the look he wanted. v1 in turn resembles the
reference. That direction of travel is a fact counsel should have.

**What I believe, again not a legal opinion.** A pale palette, a mountain, and a
scroll-driven camera are common to a great many sites and read as conventions of the
genre rather than as anyone's property. The harder question is whether the particular
*selection and arrangement* is protectable as a compilation, or functions as trade
dress. I cannot answer that.

## 5. What I am not saying

- I am not a lawyer and this is not advice.
- No lawyer has reviewed the page, the code, or this record.
- I have not searched for registered trade dress or design rights.
- I have not verified the licence terms of the elevation data beyond it being published
  as public terrain tiles. That is worth checking.
- The IP status of an image produced by a generative model is unsettled. One such image
  is in v2, as the close-range rock detail.

## 6. What I would put to counsel

0. **The repository is public and contains their compiled engine and their assets.**
   This is the first thing to decide and it does not depend on any of the others.
   Making the repository private is reversible and takes seconds.

   As of 11 August this is fixed in the *working tree* for v5 — no file of theirs is
   served by it or sits in its folder. **It is not fixed in the history.** Every one of
   those files is still reachable in past commits, with our commit history attached, and
   removing them from history needs a rewrite. A rewrite is worth doing deliberately
   rather than in a hurry, and it is the outstanding item on this point.

   It is also **not** fixed for `/cpolar/` and `/cpolar_v4/`, which still carry their
   files in the working tree. See the next question.
1. **v1 is live and serves the reference's compiled engine and about twenty of their
   asset files.** v5 now exists and is clean. Should v1 be replaced by v5 now, or taken
   down first? `/cpolar_v4/` should probably go at the same time — it is a staging step
   that still carries their stylesheet, and it serves no purpose once v5 is in place.
2. **Does reading their compiled shader to learn a technique create a problem**, given
   the measured result of one shared line of common GLSL?
3. **Is the remaining visual resemblance a risk** on a selection-and-arrangement or
   trade dress theory, and if so which specific elements should change?
4. **Is a generated rock photograph acceptable** in a commercial page.
5. **Does the elevation data need attribution.**
