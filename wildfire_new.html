Wildfire Smoke

Where the smoke is, what it does in the body, and why a filter's charge matters.

01 — Where it is

Smoke is no longer a regional problem

A single large wildfire emits fine particles in enormous numbers, and prevailing winds carry them hundreds to thousands of kilometres. Smoke from fires in the West now reaches the eastern United States and Canada on a regular basis.

02 — What it is

Most of it is smaller than 2.5 microns

Wildfire smoke is mostly soot — fine and ultrafine particles, most smaller than 2.5 micrometers across. A PM2.5 particle is roughly one-thirtieth the diameter of a human hair.5 Most of these particles carry an electric charge,1 which is how electrostatic air filters pull them from the air.

03 — What it does

Small enough to reach the blood

Particles this small deposit deep in the lungs, and a fraction pass through the alveolar wall into the bloodstream.6 The smallest particles are the ones of greatest concern.

Documented associations with wildfire-smoke fine-particle exposure:
  • ChildrenUp to about ten times the increase in pediatric respiratory visits seen with equal-mass PM2.5 from other sources. (UC San Diego, Pediatrics, 2021)7
  • PregnancyHigher odds of preterm birth. (UC Berkeley & UCSF, Environment International, 2024)8
  • CognitionLower student test scores after a school-year of exposure. (Stanford, Nature Sustainability, 2022)9
  • CardiovascularThe EPA finds a causal relationship between fine-particle exposure and heart attack and stroke. (EPA, 2019)6
04 — The toll

A rising death toll

Smoke-related fine-particle pollution is estimated to cause about 41,000 premature U.S. deaths per year (2011–2020 average), projected to reach about 71,000 per year by 2050 under a high-warming scenario — roughly 1.9 million cumulative deaths between 2026 and 2055.4

05 — The filter problem

A charge that fades under smoke

Federal guidance recommends a MERV-13 or higher filter during smoke events.11 Many are electret media: they hold a manufactured static charge that adds to mechanical filtration.

In EPA testing, a charged filter's clean-air delivery rate fell about 95 percent after roughly 10 milligrams of smoke had deposited — a few hours of a moderate event — while airflow resistance rose only about 7 percent.12 Because the pressure barely changes, there is no clog and no usual sign that the filter is spent.

A filter can rate well as MERV with its charge intact, and markedly lower as MERV-A once that charge is neutralized (ASHRAE 52.2, Appendix J).13

The question for any filter is therefore not whether it begins with a charge, but whether that charge persists under smoke loading.
