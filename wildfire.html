Wildfire smoke

What it is, where it is, and why the filter you're told to use can't keep up.

Scroll
01 / A charged particle

Wildfire smoke is mostly soot: fine and ultrafine particles, most smaller than 2.5 micrometers across. The majority of these particles carry an electric charge.[1] Air filters that use a static electric charge rely on this property to pull the particles out of the air.

02 / Scale

A single large wildfire emits these particles in very large numbers, and prevailing winds carry them hundreds to thousands of kilometres. Smoke from fires in the West now reaches the eastern United States and Canada on a regular basis.

03 / Current extent

This map shows current wildfire smoke across North America, from NOAA satellite analysis and EPA air-quality monitors.[2] The slider replays recent days; hover a location to see its measured air quality.

Wildfire smoke is no longer a regional problem. In recent years, an estimated 95 to 100 percent of the contiguous U.S. population was exposed to wildfire smoke at some point each year.[3]

The associated mortality is rising. Smoke-related fine-particle pollution is estimated to cause about 41,000 premature U.S. deaths per year (2011–2020 average), projected to reach about 71,000 per year by 2050 under a high-warming scenario — roughly 1.9 million cumulative deaths between 2026 and 2055.[4]

04 / Deposition and health

The particles of greatest concern are the smallest. A PM2.5 particle is roughly one-thirtieth the diameter of a human hair.[5] Particles this small deposit deep in the lungs, and a fraction pass into the bloodstream.[6]

Documented associations with wildfire-smoke fine-particle exposure:

  • Children — up to about ten times the increase in pediatric respiratory visits seen with equal-mass PM2.5 from other sources; largest in ages 0–5. (UC San Diego, Pediatrics, 2021)[7]
  • Pregnancy — higher odds of preterm birth. (UC Berkeley and UCSF, Environment International, 2024)[8]
  • Cognition — lower student test scores after a cumulative school-year exposure. (Stanford, Nature Sustainability, 2022)[9]
  • Cardiovascular — the EPA finds a causal relationship between fine-particle exposure and cardiovascular events, including heart attack and stroke. (EPA, 2019)[6]
  • Urban-fire smoke — after the January 2025 Los Angeles fires, researchers measured hexavalent chromium on particles smaller than 56 nanometres, up to about nine miles from cleanup zones. (UC Davis, 2026)[10]
05 / Filter performance under smoke

Federal guidance recommends a MERV-13 or higher filter during smoke events.[11] Many such filters are electret media: they hold a manufactured static charge that adds to mechanical filtration.

In EPA testing, a charged filter's clean-air delivery rate fell about 95 percent after roughly 10 milligrams of smoke had deposited — a few hours of a moderate event — while airflow resistance rose only about 7 percent.[12] Because the pressure change is small, the loss of performance produces no clog and no usual indication that the filter is spent. The charge can be restored only by washing the filter in alcohol, which is not done in practice.

Filter ratings reflect this. A filter can be rated as MERV, measured with its charge intact, and as MERV-A, measured after the electrostatic charge is neutralized (ASHRAE 52.2, Appendix J) — the second being closer to performance under real loading.[13] A filter that depends on a manufactured charge rates well as MERV and markedly lower as MERV-A.

The relevant question for any filter is therefore not whether it begins with a charge, but whether that charge persists under smoke loading.
